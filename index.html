I am a fourth-year PhD student in Finance at the University of Alabama, advised by Sandra Mortal.

I study behavioral corporate finance, real estate, and family firms. My theoretical work shows that correcting investors’ mistaken beliefs about how useful disclosure is can backfire when investors are overconfident about their future follow-through. In real estate, I find that the most actively trading REITs outperform buy-and-hold REITs by about 5.9 percentage points in next-year returns. In family firms, I examine whether family-controlled public companies are more prone to fraud.

Research

Working papers

Behavioral corporate finance

When Sophistication Backfires: Attention Overconfidence and Disclosure Design

with Byung-Cheol Kim. Presented at the FMA Annual Meeting, 2026.

Abstract

Investors who overestimate how much disclosure they can use may participate despite negative true expected surplus. Correcting this belief prevents participation driven by mistaken beliefs about usefulness at a fixed disclosure level, but can lower aggregate participant surplus once the firm responds. The firm's response depends on how the correction changes investor density at the participation cutoff. If the firm increases disclosure, it attracts investors who assess usefulness correctly but overestimate their likelihood of completing the required processing. Participant surplus falls exactly when the resulting design loss exceeds the direct gain from better participation decisions. When completion is certain, an increase in equilibrium disclosure reinforces the direct gain from correcting beliefs about usefulness.

Real estate

When Patience Backfires: Evidence from Real Estate Investment Trusts

with Sugata Ray and Mariya Letdin

Abstract

Do actively trading REITs outperform buy-and-hold REITs, and what accounts for the difference? REITs provide investors with exposure to real estate portfolios whose managers differ substantially in their property trading style. Using granular property transaction data, we find that REITs in the most active trading tercile outperform those in the least active by approximately 5.9 percentage points in next-year returns. This finding challenges the conventional wisdom that active trading necessarily reduces investor returns. We investigate whether differences in rental yields, capital gains, and selling, general, and administrative expenses can explain this performance advantage.
